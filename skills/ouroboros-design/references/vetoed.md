# Vetoed directions

Read this before proposing or building anything. These entries are bans, not the warnings in [visual direction](visual-direction.md). They come from the user rejecting real work, and they apply to every project.

A veto also covers disguised versions: the same layout reached through animation, the same motif under a new name, or the same pattern in one section instead of the whole page. Only the user can lift a veto, by asking for that exact thing in the current brief.

Rejected copy and writing patterns belong in the `ouroboros-copy` skill's vetoed file, not here. Project-specific rejections belong in that project's repository (for example `design/VETOED.md` referenced from `AGENTS.md`), not here. Check the repository for such a file too.

## Visual

- **Ruled or lined paper.** Notebook lines, margin rules, diary date blocks, paper texture, "pocket diary" layouts. It shows up on many AI-built sites. (2026-09-27)
- **The split hero and its repeating section.** A large headline on one side with an image or product shot on the other, then the same text-beside-visual block repeated for each section. This includes a page that settles into text on one side and product on the other after an animation. It reads as AI-made at a glance. (2026-09-27)
- **A stack of disconnected, wordy sections.** A page that reads as separate blocks (demo, then explainer, then feature grid, then FAQ), each with its own paragraphs, with no single thread carrying the visitor from one to the next. It reads as wordy and disconnected. Let one idea or interaction carry the whole page and let the product show itself instead of explaining. (2026-09-28)
- **Content boxed into cards.** Rounded, filled panels holding each item (a card per app, feature, or plan), laid out in a grid of equal boxes. This includes the same boxes made with a border instead of a fill. Group with type, spacing, and alignment instead. (2026-09-30)
- **Inactive controls as decoration.** Anything drawn as a control that does nothing: checkboxes that can't be checked, buttons or toggles that don't respond, fake inputs, play icons on images, or a product UI rendered on a page so it looks usable. Every element that looks interactive must work; otherwise draw it as text or leave it out. A chef never puts something on the plate that isn't meant to be eaten. (2026-09-30)
- **The massive hero.** An oversized headline filling the first screen, with a lede and a line of supporting text stacked under it, before any real content. This includes a smaller version of the same block at the top of a secondary page (a download page, pricing page, docs index). It is one of the clearest signs a site was made by AI. Open on the page's real content instead. (2026-09-30)
- **Static highlighted words.** Marker-style highlights on key words in a headline or sentence: a colored block, wash, or thick underline behind words that sit still on the page. This includes the highlight dressed up as a product cue (for example "lit like the app lights a date") and the same device made of colored text or a background chip. It is a staple of AI-driven design. (2026-09-30)
- **Turning the product site into a game.** Unless the product is a game, a marketing page framed as a game, heist, challenge or role-play (for example "you're the snoop, try to break in" with rounds, timers and scores) so the visitor could mistake the product for a game. Visitors get confused and think the product is a game. Be bold in execution, but a visitor must always see the real product doing its real job. (2026-09-28)
- **Asking visitors to leave the page.** Any interaction whose payoff requires switching tabs, blurring the window, locking the screen or otherwise leaving the site. Once a visitor's attention leaves the site, they rarely come back. (2026-09-28)
- **A first screen that needs work before it explains anything.** Pretending the site is the product (a blank editor on the page that asks the visitor to type into it) or any first screen where the visitor must act before learning what the product is and why they want it. A page has about two seconds to earn attention, and every wait spends it: intro screens, loaders, and entrance animations that hold content back all count, and each one makes the next hurt more. First-screen content renders immediately; motion can only add to what is already there. The first screen must say what it is and why it's worth it instantly, with no action; interaction can only deepen it. (2026-09-28)
- **AI-default fonts.** Instrument Serif and Space Grotesk, in any role (display, body, labels). They are to fonts what the em dash is to writing: people see them and assume AI made the site. Also avoid the other fonts models reach for unprompted on every project (for example Inter as the unexamined default, or Geist and JetBrains Mono as instant "tech" styling); choose type from the subject and say why. Before calling work done, run `scripts/check-patterns.sh`, which searches for them. (2026-10-22)
- **Glowing or gradient-ringed buttons.** Rainbow or gradient borders, outer glows, and heavy drop shadows on calls to action. It grabs attention but is a common AI-built tell. Make the primary action stand out through placement, size, contrast, and copy. (2026-10-22)
- **Content pushed to the left with the right side left empty.** A single narrow column (headline, text, form, pictures) hugging the left edge of a wide container, leaving most of the right half of a desktop screen blank, section after section. Matching a docs page's left alignment does not excuse it on a landing page. Use the width: center the opening, run related items across columns, give a picture the full container width. AI-built sites often do this because they don't use the space they are given. (2026-10-30)
- **Light-only pictures on a page that has a dark mode.** Screenshots or illustrations made only in light appearance, shown on a page that switches to dark, so bright blocks glare out of a dark page. When a page supports dark mode, make a dark version of every picture of the product and switch with `prefers-color-scheme`, or keep the page light. (2026-10-30)
- **Pills.** Capsule-shaped elements with fully rounded ends: tags, badges, status chips, filter chips, category labels, pill buttons, and capsule segmented controls. This includes the same shape made with a border instead of a fill, and a "small radius" large enough to read as a capsule. Show status, category, and metadata as plain text or color; give buttons and controls modest corners. (2026-11-17)
- **Massive white space.** Large empty areas: section gaps taller than the content they separate, a first screen or panel that is mostly blank, wide empty margins around a small block, or padding so generous the content looks lost. Content still needs breathing room, so keep spacing that separates groups and lets text read comfortably, but cut any empty area big enough to notice on its own. (2026-11-17)
- **Wide-tracked uppercase text.** Labels, headings, buttons, or captions set in all caps with extra letter spacing ("LETTER SPACING"), in any position, not only above a heading. This includes small caps and `text-transform: uppercase` paired with `letter-spacing`. Use sentence case at normal tracking. (2026-11-17)
- **Status badges with a dot.** A colored dot (often with a halo ring or pulse) beside a word like "Live", "Online", "New", "Beta", or "Active", usually inside a translucent capsule. It is the AI shorthand for "this is real-time" even when nothing on the page is. If the state is real and matters, say it in plain text where it applies. (2026-11-17)
- **Glow and spotlight backgrounds added for mood.** A soft color bloom, mesh blob, or lit spot inside a panel, card, or section that exists only to make a dark surface look atmospheric. This includes the glow at a screen edge and the purple-on-near-black version. Gradients themselves are allowed when they mean something (see the gradients section in [visual direction](visual-direction.md)); a glow "because it looks nice" is not. (2026-11-17)
- **Text over a gradient.** Any text (headings, body, labels, button labels) sitting on a gradient background, whether on a section, card, button, or a scrim over an image. This also covers gradient-filled text and text on a shader or canvas background. Put text on a solid surface; a gradient can sit beside or behind content, never under the words. (2026-11-17)
- **Icons everywhere.** An icon beside every heading, list item, feature, button, stat, or nav link, or icons standing in for words a visitor would read faster. Icons are as deliberate a choice as type and copy: use one only where it is clearer or faster than the word (a search field, a close button, a widely known platform mark), keep one family and one size, and pair it with a text label unless the meaning is universal. If a screen still works with an icon removed, remove it. (2026-11-17)
- **Teasing what needs an account.** Blurred or locked previews, "sign up to see more", padlocked features. Showing something a visitor can't have reads as a punishment for not signing up. Leave account-only things off public pages and describe the benefit in words instead. (2026-11-17)
- **Carousels that only move by arrow buttons.** People expect to swipe, drag, scroll with a trackpad, and use the keyboard. Build on native scroll-snap; Blossom Carousel handles this well (use its skill, or `npx skills add https://blossom-carousel.com`). (2026-11-17)
- **Two primary buttons in one view.** Two actions in the same primary style compete, and users can't find the one they need. Each view gets one primary action; the rest are secondary. (2026-10-22)
- **Decoration that only repeats what's next to it.** A signature device whose one working function duplicates content right beside it: a visual index or shortcut to a list directly below it, or a graphic that restates one sentence. Making the decoration clickable does not earn it a place. It only pretends to give the visitor something. (2026-11-04)
- **A borrowed palette that drops the brand's colors.** When a brief asks for inspiration from a film, artist, or genre, the brand's own colors (logo, existing marks) must stay visible in the page; the inspiration's palette can't replace them. For example, a film-inspired redesign in red and near-black for a brand whose logo is blue should still carry the blue. (2026-11-04)
- **Careless illustration drawn in code.** Scenes built from bare SVG or CSS primitives (rectangle buildings with dot-grid windows, flat towers, stiff catenary lines, tiny stick silhouettes) with no drawing intent behind them. Code-drawn art is fine when it is made with intention and care: deliberate line, shape, light and detail. Primitive placeholders posing as finished illustration read as machine-made at a glance. (2026-11-04)
- **A checklist of genre tropes as "inspiration".** Answering "inspired by Akira / Ghost in the Shell / cyberpunk" with the stock list: night skyline, neon signs, red aircraft lights, vending machines, power lines, decorative foreign-language text. That list is what any model produces for the prompt, so it signals AI. Take one specific idea from how the work was actually made or how it feels to watch, and develop it properly. Easter eggs are welcome when they are creative and fun. (2026-11-04)
- **Borrowing a culture's language the brand has no tie to.** Do not put Japanese (or any other language) text into a design because the inspiration comes from that culture, unless the brand itself has that connection. Liking the look and feel of a culture's art is not a tie between that culture and the brand. (2026-11-04)
- **Zooming or panning screenshots past their resolution.** Hover or scroll effects that scale a raster screenshot up (zoom in, push in, Ken Burns) or slide a small picture around inside a larger frame. The picture goes soft and the motion reads as cheap. Show screenshots at or below native size; if a detail matters, capture it at high resolution as its own image. (2026-11-04)
- **A solid colored border on one side of a box.** A thick or bright stripe down one edge (or one edge plus the top) of a screenshot, card, alert, notification, list row, quote, callout or panel, often as a "rim light" or accent, often curving around a rounded corner, and including a colored inset shadow on one side, sometimes paired with a hard offset shadow on the opposite side. It reads as AI-made instantly, and it doesn't look good. Frame things evenly (a hairline all round, or nothing) and let real shadow do the lifting. To mark severity or state, use a short word or the text color itself. (2026-11-04, extended 2026-11-17)
- **Generic decorative shapes that read as something else.** Abstract background shapes chosen for texture that accidentally resemble other things (a four-pane window that reads as the Windows logo, concentric rings that read as a water drop) and repeat across sections. Backgrounds should depict something from the subject itself, different for each section. (2026-11-04)
- **Public pages that look like different sites.** Docs, downloads, features, pricing or sign-in pages that each get their own page shell, navigation, type scale, spacing or content width, so the site only hangs together through the logo and colors. This includes a docs page set as a centered sign-in-style card. Every public page shares one navigation, typography, spacing and content alignment, and the menu and opening sit on the same background on every page. (2026-10-06)
- **A screenshot that could pass for the live product.** A full-width picture of the app, especially one with an input or button that looks like the page's real form, set where a visitor could try to tap or type into it. On small screens put app pictures inside a plain device outline narrower than the page's own controls, with numbered pins on the parts that matter and matching numbered notes below; on desktop, notes in the margin with thin lines to what they describe. A note must point at its part of the picture. (2026-11-04)
- **Sections with no pauses between them.** Every section the same shape on the same background with the same tight spacing, so the page reads as one run-on block. Give sections even spacing large enough to read as a pause, but not so large it becomes empty space (see the massive white space veto), and alternate full-width background bands so the eye gets a break. Vary background and spacing, not picture placement (that is the split-hero veto above). The pauses work like breaking up a run-on sentence. Also leave room between a band's edge and a picture so it doesn't sit right against the image. (2026-11-05)
- **Rotated text that has to be read.** Names, labels, headings or navigation set sideways or vertically in a language normally read horizontally, often to fit a narrow shape like a spine, capsule or column. It is hard for some people to read. Text that must be read stays horizontal; rotate only marks no one needs to read. (2026-11-04)
- **A different color for each item for no reason.** Giving each product, project, card or section its own hue in the page design (tinted frames, beams, rim lights, backgrounds) when the color means nothing to the visitor and pushes the brand color off the page. A product's own color belongs in its logo and its screenshots, not in the page built around it. (2026-11-04)
- **Recoloring product screenshots to match the page.** Tinting, duotone, film grading, color tables, filters or inverting light-mode screens so product pictures blend into the page palette. Visitors need to see what the product really looks like. Show every screen in its true colors and let the page around it carry the brand; for a dark page, capture real dark-mode screens instead of filtering light ones. (2026-11-04)
- **Imagery that suggests the wrong kind of product.** A drawing, motif or background that makes a stranger think the product is something else, such as a calendar grid behind a to-do app, which reads as a calendar app. Before keeping any illustration, ask what a visitor who has never heard of the product would guess it is from the picture alone. (2026-11-04)

## Product and layout

- **One long page of options.** A settings page, form, or feature list that runs as one long column of sections and controls. Humans are not machines; they can't scan or remember long lists of options. Break it into named groups (sidebar or tabbed sections, separate pages, or steps), show common options first, and put rarely used ones behind an "Advanced" group. Each view should hold a small set of related choices with one clear purpose. Tabs are right here because the user is looking for something specific; on marketing pages, see the tabs warning in [visual direction](visual-direction.md). (2026-10-22)
- **The main action hidden behind a click or animation.** A "Join the waitlist" button that must be clicked and slide open before the email field appears, or a sign-up behind a modal. Show the input open and ready on the first screen. The same goes for any callout: what to do should be obvious the moment it's seen, with nothing layered in front of it. Every extra step lowers conversion. (2026-10-22)
- **Containers that resize when their content changes.** A settings window, dialog, panel, popover, or card that grows or shrinks when the user switches a tab, moves to the next step, or a state changes (loading to loaded, an error appearing, a longer label). The frame jumping around the content is jarring and looks broken. Size the container once to fit its largest content, and let anything longer scroll inside it. Smaller parts follow the same rule: a button whose label changes, a counter, a status line. A disclosure the user opens may push the content below it down, but the window or panel around it keeps its size. (2026-11-18)
- **Making visitors classify themselves.** A "choose your segment" menu (Enterprise / Mid-market / Private equity…) the visitor must think about before seeing anything useful. Lead with the problems solved. (2026-10-22)

## Scroll-driven and animated pages

- **No scroll cue, or a loud one.** If the page depends on scrolling to explain itself, show a quiet visual cue at the bottom center of the first screen: a small chevron, or content visibly cut off at the bottom edge. Never a written "Scroll" label, flashing or bouncing arrows, or a sentence in the copy; people scroll on their own, and telling them to reads as AI-built. It must not cover content on small screens. (2026-09-27, revised 2026-10-22)
- **Scroll-jacking.** Taking over native scroll to drive an animation, snap between panels, or change scroll speed. People rely on the precision of normal scrolling; hijacking it frustrates them and hurts conversion. Keep native scroll; trigger animations when content enters view instead. (2026-10-22)
- **Nudging the user to do the obvious.** "Click here", "Try it", "Scroll", pulsing hints on things that are plainly interactive. AI keeps prodding; a human designer trusts the visitor. (2026-10-22)
- **Motion too fast to follow.** An animation that explains the product must be slow enough to watch the whole path, starting only once its target is fully in view. This includes changing captions and step text: each one must stay up long enough to read twice (at least about 5 seconds for a one-sentence caption), no matter how short the animation step under it is. (2026-09-27, 2026-09-28)
- **Motion faked from a few still poses.** An animation made by swapping two or three static pictures of a character or object (pose A, then B, then C), with a hop or a fade to cover the jumps. It reads as choppy, not as movement. Animate from the source (the 3D model, the vector parts, or enough rendered in-between frames to be smooth), or leave the picture still. Before building any character animation, ask whether source files exist. (2026-11-09)
- **Motion out of view on phones.** On small screens the animated action must happen inside the viewport, above other content, not behind cards or below the fold. (2026-09-27)

## Adding a veto

When the user rejects a direction, layout, motif, or interaction, before continuing:

1. Decide whether it applies to any project or only this one. If unsure, ask.
2. Add a general entry here, or a project entry in the repository's vetoed file.
3. Write what was rejected in terms another agent can recognize, the reason behind it, and the date.
4. This file is public. Paraphrase the reason instead of quoting the user, and leave out names of the user's products, clients, people, and any personal details.
