# Design lineage

Read this reference when the user asks for inspiration, names a designer or movement, or wants an opinionated critique lens. Treat lineage as a way to make decisions. Do not copy a recognizable visual style or let a name override the brief.

## Restraint and material honesty

Use the Dieter Rams lens to remove elements that do not improve usefulness, understanding, honesty, or durability. Use the Jonathan Ive lens to ask whether complexity was resolved in the product and engineering or merely hidden by a quiet surface.

Questions:

- What can be removed without making the task harder?
- Does the visible form tell the truth about behavior and consequence?
- Are design and engineering resolving the same problem?

## Constraints and iteration

Use the Charles and Ray Eames lens to treat platform, budget, content, accessibility, and production constraints as design material. Use the James Dyson lens to test uncertain mechanics and workflows with prototypes rather than polishing assumptions.

Questions:

- Which constraint can sharpen the solution?
- What assumption needs a working prototype or real-content test?
- Is the form a result of function, or styling applied after decisions were fixed?

## Standards and resilience

Use the Jeffrey Zeldman lens for semantic, open, accessible structure. Use the Dan Cederholm lens for graceful behavior when assets, fonts, networks, browsers, or content differ. Use the Jen Simmons lens for content-aware native layout rather than rigid device templates.

Questions:

- Will the structure survive redesign, assistive technology, and platform change?
- What happens when an asset, font, request, or ideal content shape fails?
- Does layout respond to content pressure or only a few breakpoints?

## Content and responsive context

Use the Karen McGrane lens to treat content models and language as product infrastructure. Use the Ethan Marcotte lens to design for fluid viewports, varied inputs, slow networks, and contexts the designer does not control.

Questions:

- Can the content move across surfaces without losing hierarchy or meaning?
- Does the design still work for people who read, tap, resize, connect, or navigate differently?

## Systems and organizations

Use the Brad Frost lens for reusable components, tokens, and a shared design-engineering language. Use the Jeffrey Veen lens when design choices affect product strategy, measurement, research, content, or team operation.

Questions:

- Can the interface be assembled from coherent reusable parts?
- Does the system help the team make later decisions, or only make this screen consistent?
- Are engineering, research, content, and product constraints visible in the direction?

## Interaction and motion

Use the Val Head lens to make motion explain causality, hierarchy, spatial relationships, or feedback. Use the Sarah Parmenter lens to account for differences in confidence, attention, and decision style.

Questions:

- What does the movement explain?
- Does the interaction remain obvious for people with different cognitive and physical needs?
- Does brand expression support the task instead of competing with it?

## Familiarity and surprise

Use the Achille Castiglioni lens to reframe familiar patterns when a product needs freshness without arbitrary novelty. Use the Chris Coyier lens to favor inspectable prototypes, practical experiments, and code another person can learn from.

Questions:

- Can a familiar pattern be adapted to the subject instead of replaced?
- Does the surprising element improve use or only display cleverness?
- Can another designer or engineer understand and extend the result?
